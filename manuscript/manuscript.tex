Age at onset in Parkinson's disease reflects inherited dose on a shared genetic
architecture\par}

\vspace{6mm}
{\small
\noindent\textbf{Background.}~Parkinson's disease beginning before 50 is widely managed, counselled
and trialled as though it were a different disease from disease beginning after 60. If the two
forms had different genetic architectures they would need different drug targets, and trials would
have to be stratified by onset age. A recent analysis reported exactly that, on the basis that
machine-learning feature rankings computed separately in each group shared almost no variants. We
asked whether age at onset in Parkinson's disease is governed by different mechanisms or by a
different dose of the same ones, and what either answer implies for target selection.\par
\vspace{1.5mm}
\noindent\textbf{Methods.}~Cohort study of 2,107 patients in the Parkinson's Progression Markers
Initiative with a recorded date of first motor symptom, of whom 403 were of inferred European
ancestry and recruited without a known pathogenic variant. We related age at onset to polygenic
burden, compared per-variant effects between patients with onset before 50 and at 60 or later,
and tested 6,259 gene sets genome-wide for onset signal conditional on risk signal under a null
that preserves genomic clustering. Effects were replicated against the International Parkinson
Disease Genomics Consortium age-at-onset study of 28,568 patients. For every implicated gene we
assessed structural tractability from its AlphaFold model against a benchmark of twenty proteins
with and without approved small-molecule drugs, and read the direction a drug would have to act in
from brain expression signals aligned onto the risk-increasing allele. We reproduced the earlier
classification analysis, and derived the error rate of its inference rule from 195,600 simulated
studies with known ground truth.\par
\vspace{1.5mm}
\noindent\textbf{Findings.}~Median age at onset was 37.0 years in carriers of a \gene{PRKN} or
\gene{PINK1} variant, 47.2 in \gene{SNCA} carriers, 59.0 in \gene{GBA} carriers, 59.7 in
\gene{LRRK2} carriers and 62.3 in patients recruited without a known variant. In that last group,
patients in the highest third of polygenic burden reached median onset 5.9 years before the lowest
third (58.0 against 63.8 years; log-rank $P$ = 0.007), and 22.4\% against 9.7\% had onset before
50. Each standard deviation of burden advanced onset by 1.48 years as the median of 24
specifications, and 85\% of risk-increasing alleles advanced onset across 86 independent loci
($r$ = $-$0.72, $P = 4.9\times10^{-15}$). No individual variant and no gene set carried onset
signal beyond its risk signal, in a test with the resolution to have found one. Held-out
prediction of an individual patient's onset age was no better than the group mean. Of seventeen
implicated genes, six carry a directional call from brain expression, and an agent acting in the
required direction exists for one. Our earlier analysis's conclusion did not survive: enrolment
flags in the standard participant-status table label 93.1\% of participants correctly with no
model fitted, study-design columns carry 41.7\% of the classifier's attribution, and the
non-overlap it relied on occurs in 42--45\% of simulated studies whose groups are identical by
construction.\par
\vspace{1.5mm}
\noindent\textbf{Interpretation.}~Early and late onset in Parkinson's disease differ in inherited
dose, not in mechanism. Stratifying trials by age at onset selects patients for burden rather than
for a distinct target, and a young patient should not be assumed to have a biologically different
illness. The genetics does carry an actionable output, but it is the direction each target must be
pushed in, which differs between two lysosomal genes that a pathway-level reading would have
treated alike.\par

the Michael J.\ Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research and its funding partners.\par}

\section*{Research in context}

\noindent\textit{Evidence before this study.}~Age at onset in Parkinson's disease is known to be
heritable and to be brought forward by rare variants in \gene{PRKN}, \gene{PINK1} and \gene{SNCA}
and, less markedly, by \gene{GBA} and \gene{LRRK2} variants. The largest genome-wide study of
onset, in 28,568 patients, reported that common risk variants also shift onset but identified few
loci acting on onset specifically. Whether early-onset disease constitutes a mechanistically
distinct entity has been argued from clinical phenotype, from imaging and, recently, from machine
learning: an analysis of the Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative reported that classifiers
trained separately in patients enrolled before 50 and at 60 or later produced feature rankings
sharing almost no variants, and nominated \gene{LRRC37A2} and \gene{GALC} as onset-specific
targets. The published resources that bear directly on the question, and that we use here, are the
IPDGC genome-wide study of age at onset in 28,568 patients, the Nalls risk meta-analysis, the
genome-wide study of blood galactosylceramidase activity of Senkevich and colleagues, GTEx v8 brain
expression, gnomAD constraint and AlphaFold DB. We are not aware of a study that reports the error
rate of the inference rule used in stratified feature-ranking comparisons, or that carries a
nominated onset target through to structural and directional assessment.

\vspace{1.5mm}
\noindent\textit{Added value of this study.}~Using age at first motor symptom rather than age at
enrolment, we show that onset falls on a single continuum from single-gene carriers to patients
with none, that polygenic burden separates the extreme thirds by 5.9 years, and that no individual
variant and no gene set carries onset signal beyond its risk signal in a test with the resolution
to have found one. We assess every implicated gene structurally against a druggability benchmark
and read the direction a drug would have to act in from brain expression aligned onto the risk
allele. We establish the operating characteristics of the ranking comparison that produced the
opposing claim, and show that the classification accuracy it rests on is carried by enrolment
design rather than by biology.

\vspace{1.5mm}
\noindent\textit{Implications of all the available evidence.}~Early and late onset should be
treated as the same disease at a different inherited dose. Stratifying trials by onset age enriches
for burden rather than selecting a distinct target, and a young patient should not be counselled as
having a biologically different illness. Where the genetics is actionable is in the sign of the
intervention, which differs between lysosomal genes that a pathway-level reading treats alike.

\section*{Main}

A neurologist who diagnoses Parkinson's disease in a patient of 45 treats that patient
differently from one of 70. Early-onset disease is coded separately, counselled differently,
enrolled into its own trials and reviewed in its own literature, and the implicit premise is that
the illness is not merely earlier but different. Whether that premise holds is not a semantic
question. It decides whether a therapy developed in one group can be expected to work in the
other, and whether age at onset is a sensible axis on which to stratify a trial.

The genetics of Parkinson's disease offers a way to settle it. Rare variants in \gene{PRKN},
\gene{PINK1} and \gene{SNCA} bring onset forward by decades, variants in \gene{GBA} and
\gene{LRRK2} by a few years, and beyond them roughly ninety common variants each shift risk by a
few per cent. If early and late onset were different diseases, the variants acting on the two
would differ. If they were the same disease at different doses, the same variants would act on
both, and the earliest patients would simply carry more of them.

Our own earlier analysis of these data, prepared as a student research manuscript and not
published, reported the first answer. It
trained gradient-boosted classifiers separately in patients enrolled before 50 and at 60 or later,
ranked variants by an explainability measure in each group, observed that the two lists shared
almost nothing, and nominated \gene{LRRC37A2} and \gene{GALC} as onset-specific targets. That
inference has an error rate, which had not been characterised, and the conclusion has consequences
for how patients are counselled and how trials are designed. We therefore did three things: asked
the clinical question directly using age at first motor symptom rather than age at enrolment,
carried the answer through to target assessment, and established what the earlier inference rule
does when the truth is known.

\subsection*{Age at onset falls on one continuum}

The clinical distinction between early and late onset is usually drawn at a cut in age, but the
genetic contribution to onset is graded rather than categorical. Among the 2,107 PPMI cases with a
recorded onset date, median onset was 37.0 years in carriers of a \gene{PRKN} or \gene{PINK1}
variant (n = 23), 47.2 in \gene{SNCA} carriers (n = 41), 59.0 in \gene{GBA} carriers (n = 108),
59.7 in \gene{LRRK2} carriers (n = 197) and 62.3 in the 1,738 cases recruited without a known
variant. Every carrier group differs from the last ($P$ = $1.2\times10^{-10}$, $4.7\times10^{-14}$, 0.0025 and
0.0012 by Mann-Whitney test), and there is no gap between the categories: the distributions overlap
throughout, and the effect of a common-variant burden described below continues the same axis into
patients who carry no known variant at all (Fig.~2b).

\subsection*{Polygenic burden and age at onset}

Age at first motor symptom is available for 2,107 PPMI cases (mean 60.5 years, s.d.\ 10.7,
range 15.4 to 92.2; 340 with onset before 50 and 1,206 after 60). Splitting the 403
inferred-European cases recruited without a known variant into thirds of polygenic burden, the
highest third reached a median onset of 58.0 years against 63.8 in the lowest, a difference of 5.9
years (log-rank $P$ = 0.007), and 22.4\% of the highest third had onset before 50 against 9.7\% of
the lowest (Fig.~2b,c). Onset falls monotonically across fifths of burden (Fig.~3d).

Among cases, each standard deviation of the META5 polygenic score was associated with earlier
onset. The pre-specified primary analysis, restricted to inferred-European sporadic cases with sex
and ten ancestry principal components (n = 403), gives 1.97 years per standard deviation (95\%
confidence interval 1.00 to 2.94, $P = 7.8\times10^{-5}$), but this is the largest of 24
specifications; the median is 1.48 years and the range extends to 0.74, with all 24 negative and
23 reaching $P < 0.05$ (Fig.~3a, Extended Data Fig.~10e). Modelled as time to event, the hazard of
onset rose by 1.22 per standard deviation (1.10 to 1.35, $P = 1.6\times10^{-4}$). Comparing cases
directly, each standard deviation raised the odds of onset before 50 rather than after 60 by 1.86
(1.34 to 2.58, $P = 2.1\times10^{-4}$; 67 against 216 cases). The two distributions of burden
overlap almost completely and differ in location alone (Fig.~4a).

Across 86 independent loci, using external data alone, the risk effect and the onset effect
correlate at $r$ = $-$0.721 ($P = 4.9\times10^{-15}$), with 85\% of risk-increasing alleles
advancing onset (Fig.~3b). Eleven of the twenty loci reaching genome-wide significance for risk
also reach $P < 0.05$ for onset. PPMI's own per-variant onset effects reproduce the IPDGC
age-at-onset study in aggregate ($r$ = +0.375 across 86 loci, $P = 3.7\times10^{-4}$,
inverse-variance weighted slope 1.63 $\pm$ 0.36; Fig.~3c) while no single variant survives
correction. At
the level of individual variants the case-case comparison gives a global heterogeneity statistic
of $\chi^2$ = 113.5 on 90 degrees of freedom ($P$ = 0.048), which is marginal and exploratory; no
individual variant approaches the threshold for the panel (Fig.~4b).

The reason nothing resolves per variant is power. At n = 403 and minor allele frequency 0.30, the
smallest onset effect detectable with 80\% power at a Bonferroni threshold is 3.53 years per
allele; the median true effect across the panel is 0.16. None of the 86 variants is detectable in
this sample.

These analyses use age at first motor symptom. Our earlier analysis stratified on age at enrolment
while describing its strata as onset cohorts; the two correlate at $r$ = 0.950, but enrolment age
exceeds true onset by a median of 2.28 years and misclassifies 6.6\% of cases at the 50-year cut,
and for 2,624 of the 3,616 participants it analysed there is no Parkinson's onset age at all. Run
on that proxy, every analysis in this section weakens or reverses (Extended Data Fig.~13).

\subsection*{Pathway analysis of onset conditional on risk}

If early and late onset were driven by different mechanisms, the pathways carrying onset signal
should differ from those carrying risk signal. Across the 86 independent risk loci, with risk
signal held fixed and significance from 20,000 label permutations, loci near lysosomal genes
showed nominally elevated onset signal ($\beta$ = +0.397, $P$ = 0.046; mean onset $\chi^2$ 4.87
within against 2.67 outside) and loci in the 17q21.31 tau region the opposite ($\beta$ =
$-$0.826; 0.31 against 3.08) (Fig.~4c). The lysosomal result does not survive scrutiny: removing
the chr4p16.3 locus raises $P$ to 0.13, only 5 of 12 single-locus removals keep $P$ below 0.05,
and 4.6\% of random 12-locus sets reach the same coefficient (Extended Data Fig.~10f). In a sweep
of 303 gene sets, none survived correction.

Genome-wide, we computed a gene-level statistic for 26,993 genes from all 6,813,424 variants in
the onset study and from the risk study, calibrated against genes of the same variant count
(genomic inflation $\lambda$ = 1.000), and tested 6,259 gene sets from five databases for onset
signal conditional on risk signal. Because neighbouring genes carry correlated statistics and gene
sets are themselves spatially clustered, significance came from circularly rotating set membership
along the genome, with multiplicity handled by the null distribution of the maximum statistic
across sets, which accounts for their overlap exactly.

No gene set reaches family-wise significance. The null of the maximum has a 95th percentile of
$t$ = 5.80, so a set at that level would have been detected; the strongest observed, high-density
lipoprotein particle at 18 genes across 13 independent loci including \gene{APOE}, reaches
$t$ = 5.18 with family-wise $P$ = 0.11 (Extended Data Fig.~5a). Every lysosomal and autophagy set
sits at family-wise $P$ = 1.00. A naive gene-permutation null that ignores clustering placed the
same lipoprotein set at a false-discovery rate of 0.001, which illustrates how much of an apparent
pathway signal can come from one genomic neighbourhood.

\subsection*{Prediction of onset age in individual patients}

Predicting onset age from the same 90 variants plus sex and ancestry components under ten-fold
nested cross-validation with hyperparameter search inside training folds, no model beat predicting
the sample mean. Held-out $R^2$ was $-$0.029 for the mean, $-$0.042 for elastic net, $-$0.052 for
gradient boosting and $-$0.443 for unregularised linear regression (Extended Data Fig.~5b). Gradient boosting
outperformed linear regression only because the latter overfits 101 predictors on 403
observations, which is a property of regularisation rather than of learning.

Screening variant pairs for interaction found nothing. Restricting to variants above 5\% minor
allele frequency and pairs with at least 30 carriers, which removes 219 pairs too sparse to fit
stably, leaves 3,151 pairs; the maximum absolute $t$-statistic is 3.98 against a permutation null
with mean 3.79 and 95th percentile 4.30 ($P$ = 0.24).

Learning curves separate the two explanations. Held-out $R^2$ for the additive score rises
monotonically from $-$0.032 at n = 100 to +0.010 at n = 403, crossing zero near n = 220. Gradient
boosting rises in parallel but from $-$0.171 to $-$0.098, and the gap does not close at any
sample size tested. The additive model is limited by sample size; the nonlinear model is limited
by the same thing and pays an additional penalty for flexibility it cannot use. The ceiling is
the variance the score explains, 3.83\%, which no architecture can exceed.

\subsection*{Structural tractability of the implicated genes}

Whether a gene can be drugged is a structural question before it is a genetic one, so we profiled
every candidate against its AlphaFold model. A grid scan for buried cavities recovered the
annotated catalytic site as the largest pocket in \gene{GALC} (1,287~\AA$^3$, containing all six
UniProt-annotated active and binding residues), \gene{GBA1} (1,050~\AA$^3$, both catalytic
glutamates), \gene{GAK}, \gene{APOE} and \gene{LRRK2}, which is the check that the scan is finding
real sites rather than surface dimples.

To score rather than describe those descriptors we ran the same pipeline on twenty control
proteins, ten carrying an approved small-molecule drug and ten long-standing hard targets. Pocket
enclosure separated them at an area under the curve of 0.96, ahead of hydrophobicity of the lining
(0.83), mean predicted confidence (0.84), fraction of disordered residues (0.79) and pocket volume
(0.74). At the best cut, 0.705, nine of ten drugged and ten of ten undrugged controls are placed
correctly.

Applied to the candidates, the structural facts are specific. \gene{GALC} has a confidently
predicted fold (mean confidence 94.6) and a well-formed catalytic pocket, is expressed in the
substantia nigra at 12.1 transcripts per million, and is tolerant of loss of function in healthy
people (LOEUF 0.94); what it does not have is a single experimental structure in the Protein Data
Bank, with or without a ligand. Its obstacle is chemistry, not structure. \gene{LRRC37A2} has no
fold to target: mean predicted confidence 42.3, with 77\% of residues below the threshold at which
a predicted structure carries information, so the cavity the scan reports in it is an artefact of
a disordered chain. \gene{SNCA} has no pocket above 50~\AA$^3$ at all, which is why the agents
against it target the aggregate rather than the monomer (Fig.~6).

The benchmark bounds itself. Two candidates with dozens of ligand-bound structures, \gene{GBA1}
and \gene{CTSB}, fall below the enclosure cut, because a glycosidase substrate channel is wide
where a kinase or a carbonic anhydrase site is tight. Enclosure is therefore informative across
protein classes and unreliable within the lysosomal hydrolases specifically, and the decisive
practical fact for that class remains whether a ligand has ever been crystallised in the site.

\subsection*{Direction of effect at each locus}

A target is only useful with a direction attached. For each candidate we took every significant
brain expression signal for the gene, lifted it to the coordinates of the risk study, aligned the
effect onto the risk-increasing allele, and kept the signals at the gene's own risk locus; each of
the 13 brain regions then voted once, using the median of its own variants, so that a
region contributing thousands of records cannot outvote the rest. That gives 39,694 aligned
records.

Six genes carry a directional call. The risk allele lowers \gene{LRRC37A2} in all 13 regions and
\gene{TMEM175} in 3 of 3, so a drug would have to raise them; it raises \gene{GPNMB} in 11 of 11,
\gene{GAK} in 2 of 2 and \gene{GALC} in 2 of 2, so a drug would have to lower them. \gene{MAPT} is
mixed. The \gene{GALC} call agrees with the linkage-based analysis reported above, in which all
five brain expression signals in high linkage disequilibrium with the disease lead variant raise
the gene. Of the six, an agent acting in the required direction exists for one, \gene{GPNMB}
(Fig.~7a,b).

The direction matters more than it appears. \gene{GBA1} and \gene{GALC} are both lysosomal
hydrolases and both are lysosomal storage disease genes, but the genetics point opposite ways:
loss of glucocerebrosidase raises risk, so ambroxol is given to raise it, whereas raised
galactosylceramidase appears to raise risk, so the intervention would be inhibition. A programme
that treated the lysosomal loci as one mechanism would get the sign wrong for one of them.

\subsection*{Galactosylceramidase activity and disease risk}

Our earlier analysis nominated \gene{GALC} on the basis of rs8005172, described as the only
coding variant among those selected, with a gain-of-function mechanism in which the substitution
stabilises a loop at the edge of the binding pocket. That variant carries PPMI's own annotation
\pep{GALC/GPR65}, and Ensembl returns \pep{intron\_variant} assigned to \gene{GPR65}. It changes
no amino acid, so there is no substituted residue and the conformational mechanism cannot be
evaluated. Across the 90-variant panel only four variants are missense and 64 are intronic. Of the
25 strongest Parkinson's disease variants at the \gene{GALC} locus, none is coding (Extended Data Fig.~16a).

The underlying hypothesis is separable from that error and is due to Senkevich and colleagues,
who report that rs979812 raises blood galactosylceramidase activity and that Mendelian
randomisation places raised activity causally upstream of Parkinson's disease. rs979812-T raises
disease risk ($\beta$ = 0.061, 95\% confidence interval 0.043 to 0.079, $P = 6\times10^{-11}$)
and raises enzyme activity ($\beta$ = 1.205, 1.09 to 1.32, $P = 5\times10^{-95}$, n = 1,454), with
a Mendelian randomisation estimate of 0.025 (s.e.\ 0.007, $P = 8\times10^{-4}$) (Fig.~7d).

If the mechanism is that the risk haplotype raises \gene{GALC} expression, then in an independent
tissue and an independent assay the same haplotype should raise \gene{GALC} transcript abundance.
That is falsifiable and could have failed. Among GTEx brain expression quantitative trait loci for
\gene{GALC} in linkage disequilibrium with the risk variant at $r^2 > 0.6$, all five raise
\gene{GALC} expression when aligned to the risk allele, with a maximum $r^2$ of 0.992 and a median
aligned effect of +0.220 (Fig.~7e). The direction is concordant.

Two limits qualify this. Colocalisation between the disease signal and the strongest cerebellar
expression signal favours distinct causal variants (posterior probability 0.046 for one shared
variant against 0.927 for an expression signal only), although the deposited disease statistics
for this locus are substantially weaker than the published values and the comparison is
underpowered rather than negative. And \gene{GALC} is under detectable genetic control in only 4 of
13 GTEx brain tissues, dominated by cerebellum and cerebellar hemisphere, and absent from
substantia nigra, putamen, caudate and cortex (Extended Data Fig.~16b).

\subsection*{Structure prediction at the \gene{GALC} locus}

We folded \gene{GALC} wild type against 53 single substitutions with ESMFold, comprising 22 ClinVar
pathogenic, 13 gnomAD common and 18 random variants. Fifty-two of the 53 predicted mutant
structures sit within 1~\AA{} C$\alpha$ root-mean-square deviation of wild type, and the largest
change in mean predicted confidence across all 53 is 1.03 pLDDT points (Extended Data Fig.~12).

Four measures were compared for separating pathogenic from tolerated variants. Distance to the
catalytic pair E198 and E274, measured on the AlphaFold model of P54803, gives an area under the
curve of 0.823 ($P$ = 0.002); change in mean pLDDT gives 0.780 ($P$ = 0.007); root-mean-square
deviation between predicted mutant and wild-type structures gives 0.706 ($P$ = 0.046); and pLDDT at
the residue itself gives 0.323 (Extended Data Fig.~6). Structure prediction is therefore informative for this
protein, but through geometry rather than through comparison of predicted mutant structures.

None of these measures predicts the direction of an activity change, because each scores
disruption, and disruption is a loss-of-function signal by construction. No coding variant that
raises galactosylceramidase activity has been reported, so there is no substitution on which a
gain-of-function structure prediction could be attempted, by this or any method.

\subsection*{The 17q21.31 locus}

The early-onset nomination rested on rs62053943, which falls inside \gene{LRRC37A2}. The variant
sits in a polymorphic inversion. In 525 unrelated European samples from the 1000 Genomes Project,
the haplotype carrying it extends 620~kb at $|D'| \geq 0.9$ across \gene{CRHR1}, \gene{LRRC37A2},
\gene{MAPT} and \gene{KANSL1}, and 1,312 common variants sit at $r^2 \geq 0.5$, \gene{MAPT} among
them (Extended Data Fig.~7). Expression data does not separate them either: the variant is a significant brain
expression quantitative trait locus for 25 genes across all 13 GTEx brain tissues, and the
\gene{MAPT}-annotated variant for 28, almost the same set.

The locus is not weak. It ranks fifth of the 86 panel loci for disease risk. It ranks 77th for
onset. What fails is the gene assignment and the onset framing, not the locus.

Ranking the fifteen candidate genes with a gene-based onset statistic separates them cleanly from
the two the earlier analysis nominated. \gene{SNCA} is the only gene reaching genome-wide
significance for onset; \gene{TMEM175}, \gene{GAK}, \gene{DGKQ}, \gene{IDUA} and \gene{APOE}
follow. Four of the fifteen have an agent already in clinical development and a further seven have
a ligand-bound structure. \gene{GALC} and \gene{LRRC37A2} rank ninth and thirteenth, and neither
has small-molecule tractability evidence in Open Targets (Extended Data Fig.~15).

\subsection*{Classification accuracy in PPMI}

We examined an application in which gradient-boosted classifiers separated four clinical
categories within age strata at weighted F1 of 0.95 to 0.96, and non-overlapping SHAP-selected
variant lists were read as distinct genetic architecture.

PPMI's participant-status table carries enrolment flags recording which sub-study each
participant entered: \pep{ENRLNORM} for healthy controls, \pep{ENRLRBD} and \pep{ENRLHPSM} for
the prodromal cohorts, \pep{ENRLSRDC} for sporadic disease, and \pep{ENRLLRRK2}, \pep{ENRLGBA},
\pep{ENRLSNCA}, \pep{ENRLPRKN} and \pep{ENRLPINK1} for the genetic cohorts. Assigning each
participant the majority cohort of their flag pattern, with no model fitted, is correct for
93.1\% of 7,545 participants across 43 distinct patterns. Ten-fold cross-validation gives 92.9\%
(s.d.\ 0.8\%) against a majority-class baseline of 67.6\%, so this is not an overfitting artefact
(Extended Data Fig.~7).

Under $5\times5$-fold repeated stratified cross-validation with blocks assigned by data
provenance, the twenty enrolment-design columns alone reached weighted F1 0.953 against 0.956 for
all 862 numeric features in the late stratum, and 0.841 against 0.827 in the early stratum, where
the design block alone outperformed the full model. Removing that block dropped the full model to
0.749 and 0.641. The 162 genotype columns alone reached 0.644 against a majority-class baseline
of 0.498, and 0.573 against 0.532 (Fig.~8, Extended Data Fig.~4). A classifier given only binary indicators of which
measurements a participant has, with no feature values at all, reached F1 0.759 and area under the
curve 0.912, which is better than any model built from the genetic data, whose best was 0.724.

Ascertainment also differs between the compared strata. Of 1,152 cases with a polygenic score and
an enrolment age, 285 (24.7\%) were recruited because they carry a pathogenic variant, 31.9\% in
the early stratum against 23.5\% in the late (odds ratio 1.52, Fisher $P$ = 0.053).

\subsection*{Model attributions}

Ablation removes a block and asks what the accuracy does. The complementary question is what the
fitted model attends to when nothing is removed, which is what a feature-importance ranking is
meant to answer, so we refitted the three model families on the corrected matrix and read their
Shapley attributions.

In the late stratum the three largest attributions are enrolment flags: the hyposmia arm (1.013),
the sporadic arm (0.671) and the REM-sleep arm (0.427), an order of magnitude above the next
feature. Fourth is the count of pathogenic variants the study itself recorded (0.246) and sixth
the number of visits at which RNA sequencing was performed (0.194); neither is a measurement of
the patient's biology, and both record how the study handled them. Study-design columns carry
41.7\% of all attribution in the late stratum and 21.6\% in the early stratum, against 13.1\% and
15.3\% for genotypes and 1.9\% and 3.2\% for polygenic scores. No genotype feature enters the top
twenty of the late stratum at all; one does in the early stratum (Fig.~5a,b).

Resampling the patients 200 times and refitting separates what is stable from what is not. The
three enrolment flags hold ranks one, two and three in every resample. Below them the ordering is
unstable: the tau sub-study indicator, tenth in the full sample, ranges from tenth to
forty-eighth, and the ancestry component ranked eighteenth stays in the top twenty in 36\% of
resamples against 25\% for the one ranked twenty-first (Fig.~5c, Extended Data Fig.~14a,b). A ranking whose leading entries are fixed by study
design and whose remainder moves this much cannot support a claim about biological architecture.

The strongest single genotype in the late stratum makes the point in miniature. It is missing for
2,627 of 3,386 patients, because whether a participant was typed on that array depends on which
sub-study they joined, and the model assigns those patients an attribution of their own rather
than treating the value as absent (Extended Data Fig.~14c).

\subsection*{Overlap between variant lists within a single group}

Splitting the late-onset stratum at random into subsets matching the two published strata, so
that architecture is identical by construction, and running the earlier selection procedure on
each, gave a mean overlap of 1.21 variants across 3,600 replicates, with zero overlap in 27.0\%.
The observed cross-stratum overlaps were 2, 2 and 0 for the three model families, and 89.1\% of
replicates under known-identical architecture returned an overlap at or below the observed
maximum (Extended Data Fig.~3).

\subsection*{Operating characteristics of the ranking comparison}

We simulated 195,600 two-stratum association studies across 978 parameter combinations, varying
the smaller stratum from 100 to 2,000 against a fixed 3,386, the candidate panel across 50, 162
and 500 variants, the causal count across 5 and 15, per-allele odds ratios from 1.05 to 1.49,
linkage disequilibrium block structure, and the compared list length across $k$ = 5, 10, 20 and
30.

Four generative scenarios were used. Under \textit{identical}, both strata carry the same causal
variants with the same effects. Under \textit{dose}, the same variants with effects halved in one
stratum, which is one architecture at two intensities. Under \textit{half shared}, half the
causal variants are common. Under \textit{none shared}, none is. A method that tests for distinct
architecture should reject rarely under the first two and often under the last two. Each
replicate was analysed twice: with the ranking rule, fitting gradient-boosted trees separately in
each stratum, ranking by mean absolute SHAP and declaring distinct architecture when the top-$k$
lists did not intersect; and with a formal test, estimating each variant's effect separately in
each stratum, testing the difference by a $z$-test and combining across variants.

At the operating point of a typical complex-disease study, 230 against 3,386 with 162 candidate
variants, $k$~=~10 and per-allele odds ratios of 1.05 to 1.11, the ranking rule concluded
``distinct architecture'' in 45.4\% and 42.2\% of replicates whose architectures were identical.
Where the strata shared no causal variant it concluded the same in 47.3\% and 46.8\%
(Extended Data Fig.~1a). The difference between those rates, which is the rule's entire information content,
is 0.019 and 0.045. The formal test returned 6.6\% and 7.0\% under identical architecture and
7.4\% and 12.0\% under disjoint architecture (Extended Data Fig.~1b). Neither has appreciable power at these
effect sizes, which is the correct answer, because none is available. The rule reports a positive
finding in nearly half of those studies and the formal test reports nothing.

At larger effects the two separate. At an odds ratio of 1.49 the ranking rule had a false-positive
rate of 0.054 and power of 0.560 against disjoint architecture; the formal test had 0.064 and
0.759.

The ranking rule's false-positive rate does not improve with sample size: 0.482 with 100 in the
smaller stratum against 0.426 with 2,000 (Extended Data Fig.~2a, Extended Data Fig.~8). The formal test held between 0.032 and 0.112
across every cell (Extended Data Fig.~2b), although its size is not uniform: 0.043 under independence and 0.091
when variants sit in correlated blocks, because combining per-variant statistics as $\Sigma z^2$
against $\chi^2$ assumes independence.

\subsection*{Dependence on the length of the compared list}

On data whose architecture is identical, the probability of concluding ``distinct'' was 0.752 at
$k$~=~5, 0.302 at $k$~=~10, 0.035 at $k$~=~20 and 0.000 at $k$~=~30 (Extended Data Fig.~1c), with mean overlap
rising from 0.28 to 6.69 variants. Whether a study reports distinct architectures is therefore
determined by how many features the analyst chooses to list, a parameter rarely justified and
never varied in sensitivity analysis.

\section*{Discussion}

Early and late onset in Parkinson's disease differ in how much inherited risk the patient carries,
not in which mechanisms are at work. The evidence for that is consistent across every level at
which it can be examined. Onset falls on one continuum from \gene{PRKN} and \gene{PINK1} carriers
through \gene{SNCA}, \gene{GBA} and \gene{LRRK2} to patients with no known variant, with no gap
between the categories. Within the last of those groups, polygenic burden moves onset by 5.9 years
between the extreme thirds. Across 86 loci the alleles that raise risk are the alleles that advance
onset, at $r$ = $-$0.72. And at the level where a mechanistic difference would have to appear,
neither an individual variant nor any of 6,259 gene sets carries onset signal beyond its risk
signal, in a test whose resolution was sufficient to have found one.

For the clinic this changes what age at onset can be taken to mean. A patient diagnosed at 45 is
not thereby a patient with a different illness, and should not be counselled as though the biology
were different; what distinguishes them, on average, is that they carry more of the same risk. For
trials it changes what stratification by onset age achieves. It enriches for burden, which is a
legitimate design choice if burden is the modifier of interest, but it does not select for a
distinct target, and a therapy that works in one group has no genetic reason to fail in the other.
The corollary is that the genetic evidence does not license two development programmes where one
would do.

Onset age is nonetheless not predictable for an individual. Held-out prediction from genetics was
no better than the group mean, and the learning curves show why: the additive signal is small and
the nonlinear one is absent, so this is not a sample-size problem that a larger cohort will solve.
Polygenic burden is a description of a group, not a prognosis for a patient, and should not be
presented to one as though it were.

Where the genetics does become actionable is direction. A target is only useful with a sign
attached, and the sign is not a property of the pathway. \gene{GBA1} and \gene{GALC} are both
lysosomal hydrolases, both storage disease genes, and both sit in the lysosomal cluster a
pathway-level reading would treat as one mechanism; but loss of glucocerebrosidase raises risk,
which is why ambroxol is given to raise it, whereas raised galactosylceramidase appears to raise
risk, so the intervention would be inhibition. Reading the pathway rather than the allele gets the
sign wrong for one of the two. Of the seventeen genes we assessed, six carry a directional call
from brain expression and an agent acting the required way exists for one.

Structural assessment separates two different reasons a target can be unavailable. \gene{GALC} has
a confidently predicted fold, a catalytic pocket the scan recovers with all six annotated site
residues, expression in the substantia nigra and tolerance of loss of function in healthy people;
what it lacks is any experimental structure at all. Its obstacle is chemistry, and chemistry is
tractable. \gene{LRRC37A2} is 77\% disordered and has no fold to bind, which is a different kind of
answer, and \gene{SNCA} has no pocket above 50~\AA$^3$, which is why the agents against it address
the aggregate rather than the monomer.

The \gene{GALC} result is the constructive counterpart. A hypothesis can be correct while the
argument offered for it is not, and the two require separate verdicts. Raised galactosylceramidase
activity as a cause of Parkinson's disease survives an independent directional test that could
have refuted it. What does not survive is a conformational mechanism asserted for a variant that
changes no residue, and the confusion is instructive: the disease signal at this locus is entirely
regulatory, so a structural method was the wrong instrument for a target that may well be real.
Applied to the geometry of the enzyme rather than to comparison of predicted mutant structures,
the same tool is the best-performing of the four we compared.

Onset-modifying targets should therefore be sought among the loci that carry onset signal, which
are \gene{SNCA}, the chr4p16.3 lysosomal cluster comprising \gene{TMEM175}, \gene{GAK},
\gene{DGKQ} and \gene{IDUA}, and \gene{APOE}, rather than among the outputs of a stratified
ranking. A therapeutic hypothesis resting on a specific molecular mechanism also requires evidence
at that resolution: a locus tagging a 620~kb inversion cannot nominate one of its genes, and a
non-coding variant cannot support a claim about protein conformation.

Our earlier analysis reached the opposite conclusion for reasons that generalise past this dataset.
Feature importance is a property of a fitted model, not of the data-generating process, and its
rank is a high-variance functional of that model; comparing ranks between two models fitted to
different samples inherits the variance of both, and nothing in the procedure propagates it. On
data whose two groups are identical by construction, the comparison reports ``distinct'' in 42 to
45\% of studies, and its discrimination between identical and disjoint architecture is 0.019 to
0.045. The remedy is not a better importance measure but stating the hypothesis as a parameter and
testing it: distinct architecture is a claim about effect heterogeneity, and a heterogeneity test
has a null distribution, an effect size and a confidence interval.

The PPMI observations have a scope beyond the study that prompted them. PPMI is among the most
heavily used cohorts in machine-learning studies of Parkinson's disease, and the enrolment flags
sit in the same standard table as the outcome label. Any supervised model trained on that table
without excluding them is partly a model of recruitment. The same holds for measurement
availability: in this cohort, which assays a participant has predicts diagnostic group better than
the assay results do.

\subsection*{Limitations}

The simulations model additive effects on a binary outcome with block linkage disequilibrium; real
architectures include rare variants, epistasis and gene-environment interaction not represented,
although the interaction screen in the real data found nothing. The heterogeneity test we compare
against is a simple combination of per-variant statistics and is inflated under linkage
disequilibrium; better-powered and better-calibrated tests exist. The gene-based analysis uses a
minimum-$P$ statistic calibrated by variant count rather than an explicit linkage disequilibrium
model, which the rotation null mitigates but does not remove.

PPMI's genetic subset is small and predominantly European. The 403 cases in the primary analysis
match the excluded cases on onset age (60.1 against 60.6 years, $P$ = 0.36) and on the proportion
with onset before 50, but are enriched for family history (0.531 against 0.394) even after
removing declared carriers, which could bias the polygenic association upward. Onset age is
self-reported symptom onset, correlating with age at diagnosis at $r$ = 0.95 with a median
diagnostic delay of 1.0 year; recall error is not quantified. Whether PPMI contributed to the
IPDGC onset study could not be established: the deposited file records 17 contributing studies
without naming them, so partial sample overlap in the replication cannot be excluded.

The deposited Nalls summary statistics exclude a large contributing cohort, so per-locus
significance in our regional analyses is weaker than the published values; effect sizes agree. We
report published values where they exist and mark deposited values as such. Across this work we
ran, among others, 225 ablation arms, 90 per-variant onset tests, 303 locus-level gene sets, 6,259
genome-wide gene sets, 3,151 interaction pairs and 24 polygenic specifications; the two nominally
significant exploratory findings sit inside that, and neither is defensible as a discovery. A full
adversarial audit, including four claims weakened on re-examination and two reported numbers
corrected, is given in Extended Data Figs.~10 and 11.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Simulation}

Genotypes were drawn under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium with minor allele frequencies uniform on
(0.05, 0.5), optionally with five-variant linkage blocks induced through a latent normal with
correlation 0.6. Binary case status came from a logistic model on the causal set. The ranking rule
fitted an XGBoost classifier (200 trees, depth 4, learning rate 0.05) in each stratum, computed
mean absolute SHAP values and compared the top-$k$ sets. The formal test fitted a per-variant
logistic regression in each stratum, formed $z = (\beta_1 - \beta_2)/\sqrt{\mathrm{SE}_1^2 +
\mathrm{SE}_2^2}$ and combined across variants as $\Sigma z^2$ against $\chi^2$ on the number of
variants tested. 978 parameter cells $\times$ 200 replicates were executed on Modal.

\subsection*{The earlier analysis}

The analysis referred to throughout as ours and earlier is an unpublished student research
manuscript prepared by two of the present authors, ``Machine learning defines distinct genetic
architectures in early- and late-onset Parkinson's disease and ranks targets for small-molecule
therapy''. It classified PPMI participants into four clinical groups within strata defined by age
at enrolment, ranked variants by mean absolute Shapley value separately in each stratum, reported
that the two lists of 10 and 13 variants did not intersect, and nominated \gene{LRRC37A2} and
\gene{GALC}. It was never submitted and is not in the literature; we describe it here because the
present work began as an attempt to confirm it, and because its data, code and intermediate files
were available to us in full. Its matrix, notebooks and reported tables are the input to every
reproduction reported below.

\subsection*{Cohort data}

PPMI subject-characteristics release of 15 August 2026 and \pep{PD\_Diagnosis\_History} of 16
August 2026: participant status (9,050), polygenic risk scores (2,996), Project 9001 dosages and
ten ancestry principal components (1,319), demographics, family history, socio-economics, and the
2018 whole-genome pathogenic-variant panel (959). Age at onset is the interval from \pep{BIRTHDT}
to \pep{SXDT}; age at diagnosis uses \pep{PDDXDT}. \pep{COHORT\_x} and \pep{COHORT\_y} are numeric
encodings of the outcome and were excluded from all feature matrices.

Ablation used $5\times5$-fold repeated stratified cross-validation over blocks assigned by
provenance: enrolment design (20), genetics-core carrier calls (2), digital gait and wearable
(498), family history (108), demographics (25), age (31), genotypes (162), polygenic scores (6),
ancestry principal components (10). Models were XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost with matched
hyperparameters against a majority-class baseline. Cloud experiments used a de-identified numeric
matrix with participant identifiers and dates removed.

Association analyses used ordinary least squares for onset age on standardised polygenic score or
per-variant dosage, adjusted for sex and ten ancestry principal components, restricted to
inferred-European participants with the genetically ascertained cohort removed; Cox proportional
hazards for the time-to-event formulation; and logistic regression of early- against late-onset
membership for the case-case comparison. Multiple testing by Benjamini-Hochberg.

\subsection*{Pathway analysis}

Loci were annotated positionally against RefSeq hg19 with a 100~kb window. The locus-level
competitive test regressed $\log(1 + \text{onset }\chi^2)$ on set membership with
$\log(1 + \text{risk }\chi^2)$ as covariate, with significance from 20,000 permutations of the
membership vector. Genome-wide, a minimum-$P$ statistic was computed for every RefSeq gene from
variants within 35~kb, calibrated to a rank within 30 bins of log variant count, and gene sets
from KEGG, Reactome, Gene Ontology and WikiPathway with 10 to 500 mapped genes were tested for
onset signal conditional on risk signal. Test statistics were computed in closed form by
Frisch-Waugh-Lovell, and significance came from 20,000 circular rotations of the membership vector
along the genome with family-wise correction from the null of the maximum statistic across sets.

\subsection*{GALC analyses}

Regional summary statistics were taken for chr14:88.36--88.58~Mb (GRCh37). Variant consequences
came from Ensembl VEP on GRCh37. GTEx v8 significant variant-gene pairs were used for all 13 brain
tissues. Linkage disequilibrium and the sign of $D$ were computed from 1000 Genomes 30$\times$
GRCh38 phased haplotypes restricted to 525 unrelated Europeans, and expression effects were
aligned onto the risk allele using the sign of $D$. Colocalisation used Wakefield approximate
Bayes factors with prior probabilities $10^{-4}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-5}$. Structure prediction
used ESMFold (\pep{facebook/esmfold\_v1}) on A100-40GB GPUs with C$\alpha$ root-mean-square
deviation by Kabsch superposition; catalytic and binding-site residues and the reference model
were taken from UniProt P54803 and AlphaFold DB.

\subsection*{Attribution and its stability}

The three gradient-boosted models were refitted on the corrected matrix within each stratum and
interrogated with tree Shapley values, summed over classes and averaged over patients, so that
each feature carries a single non-negative attribution on the scale of the model's output. Total
attribution was apportioned to the provenance blocks defined for the ablation. To ask how much of
the resulting ranking is a property of the data rather than of the particular patients, the
stratum was resampled with replacement 200 times, the model refitted and the attributions
recomputed on each resample, and every feature's rank recorded; we report the median rank, the
fifth to ninety-fifth percentile, and the proportion of resamples in which the feature remains in
the top twenty.

\subsection*{Structure and pocket detection}

Predicted structures were taken from AlphaFold DB through its prediction API, one model per
UniProt accession. Cavities were found with a grid scan in the manner of LIGSITE: the protein was
placed on a 0.8~\AA\ grid, points clearing every atom's van der Waals radius plus a 1.4~\AA\ probe
were marked free, and a free point was scored one protein-solvent-protein event for each of seven
scan directions along which protein was met in both directions within 10~\AA. Free points with at
least four of seven events were retained, connected components of at least 50~\AA$^3$ formed
pockets, and each pocket was described by its volume, its enclosure (mean events divided by seven),
the residues within 5~\AA\ of any pocket point, and the fraction of those residues that are
hydrophobic. Active and binding residues came from the UniProt feature table for the same
accession, and a pocket was called catalytic if it contained at least one of them. Structures
above 90 million grid cells were rescanned at 1.2~\AA. The benchmark set comprised ten proteins
carrying an approved small-molecule drug (\gene{EGFR}, \gene{HMGCR}, \gene{PTGS2}, \gene{ACE},
\gene{DHFR}, \gene{CA2}, \gene{ADRB2}, \gene{BRAF}, \gene{PARP1}, \gene{HDAC2}) and ten proteins
long regarded as hard (\gene{MYC}, \gene{TP53}, \gene{CTNNB1}, \gene{STAT3}, \gene{NPM1},
\gene{MAX}, \gene{JUN}, \gene{FOXO3}, \gene{RUNX1}, \gene{MECP2}), run through the identical
pipeline. Experimental structure counts and the subset holding a non-polymer entity came from the
RCSB search API; tractability categories from the Open Targets Platform; loss-of-function
intolerance from gnomAD v2.1.1 constraint; brain expression from the GTEx v8 median expression
service.

\subsection*{Direction of effect}

For each candidate we took the strongest risk-associated variant within 500~kb of the gene as the
locus lead and oriented it onto its risk-increasing allele. Every significant brain expression
signal for that gene in all 13 GTEx v8 brain tissues was lifted from GRCh38 to GRCh37 with the
UCSC chain file, matched to the risk statistics by position, and kept only where the two allele
pairs agreed; the expression slope was then flipped where GTEx's alternate allele was not the risk
allele. Signals were restricted to within 250~kb of the lead and to a risk $P$-value within two
orders of magnitude of the lead's own, so that a weak locus is judged on its own scale. Each
tissue contributed one vote, the median slope of its retained variants. A direction was called
where at least three quarters of voting tissues agreed, and graded strong where at least five
tissues agreed at 90\% or above, moderate at three tissues and 75\%, and weak otherwise.

\subsection*{External data}

IPDGC age-at-onset GWAS (GWAS Catalog GCST007780; 28,568 cases; 6,813,424 variants, GRCh37); Nalls
META5 risk GWAS (GCST009325, deposited release); GWAS Catalog curated associations for published
values; 1000 Genomes 30$\times$ GRCh38 phased panel; GTEx v8; Ensembl VEP and variation REST;
UniProt P54803; ClinVar; gnomAD; AlphaFold DB; Open Targets Platform.

external datasets are public. Analysis scripts, per-fold results, supplementary tables and vector
